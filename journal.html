Apex Out Run · project journal

From kart track to coast-to-coast run

Apex Run rebuilt as an Out Run highway simcade: one minute per stage, a fork at the end of each, and five endings.

The app now is a playable Out Run on the deployed page. You start at a title card and get a 3-2-1 grid. Then you drive a four-lane highway through 15 stages, each with its own light, haze and roadside, and choose a branch at every fork against a 75 s clock that each checkpoint extends by 45 s, to live synthesized engine sound and a three-station FM radio. Escape pauses the race. A ranking run takes three initials onto a shared live board, and two people can race a duel on one keyboard.

The target

The Stitch "Coast 2 Coast" frame is the visual oracle (DESIGN.md, accepted on 2026-10-16): golden-hour light, palm rows, a multi-lane highway into haze, and telemetry at the corners.

Turn 41 · The end of the road: each ending's destination, and your road trip

Results. Finishing the last stage no longer drives the car into the void. Past the final arch the road runs on into the ending's own place, the car drives itself in and stops, and then the game replays the player's own run before the results.

  • The arrival. Each of the five endings has its destination at the road's end: the harbour clock tower, the kasbah, the castle's keep between two gate towers, the colosseum and the airship hangar. The car eases off, brakes and pulls up in front of it, the camera rises to frame the place over the car, and the riders wave. It lasts about eight seconds, with the HUD out of the way. A test drives every ending through the finish and checks, frame by frame, that there is road under the car and land on both sides of it.
  • The girl waves. The art pack bakes both of her arms onto the wheel. The game now cuts her outer arm out when the car is loaded, so she can lift it and wave as the boy does.
  • Your road trip. A seven-second clip of each stage raced, around its landmark, cut in three: a helicopter shot, the chase camera's landmark pull, and a camera at the roadside as the car goes by. It is letterboxed, with YOUR ROAD TRIP and each stage's name over it, and the radio keeps playing. Enter or the gamepad's confirm skips it.
  • It is your run. The clips are rebuilt from the run's own saved milestones, not from an AI driver. Each stage's landmark is now a milestone that saves the race's state, so a clip starts instantly; the inputs saved with the next milestone play it forward. A test checks that every replayed frame matches the original race exactly.
  • Fixed on the way. When the frame rate allowed the picture to return to full resolution, the screen went black from then on; the road trip's light frames made it happen every time. The title, pause and pit-lane text was half Portuguese; it now reads in English like the rest of the game.
  • Not changed. Time over goes straight to its results, and an online duel keeps its end card: a duel saves no landmark milestones, so it has no road trip.

Turn 40 · Two racers online: a lobby, a challenge, and a duel the machine signals and decides

Results. The eighth slice of the kinetic-machine record is built. Two people in two browsers meet in a lobby, one challenges the other on a stage of their choosing, and they race it against each other over a direct connection between the two browsers. The server never carries the race; it introduces the two browsers to each other and then records the result both of them report.

  • The lobby. The title card's DUELO ONLINE opens it. A racer goes online under a callsign, sees who else is there, picks one of the ten road stages and challenges; the other accepts or declines. It is Truco's lobby, rebuilt as Apex Run's own machines: presence while a tab is open, and challenges that expire after thirty seconds.
  • The duel machine. Accepting opens a duel on the server. Its rows carry each browser's connection details to the other, so no separate signalling service is needed. During the race each browser reports its milestones — the grid, the start, the finish — and the machine pairs the two reports of each one. If the two agree, the finish is the result. If they disagree, the duel is marked disputed. If one racer goes quiet, the duel is abandoned and the quiet racer is named.
  • The race over the wire. The arena's race now has an online mode. Each browser predicts the other car's input and rolls the race back when the real input arrives, using GGRS, which Turn 37 brought in. A milestone is reported only once both browsers' inputs for its frame have arrived, so the two reports can be compared.
  • The end card. It shows the result as the machine recorded it: you won, you lost, a draw, disputed, or abandoned. The lobby is a click away.
  • Checked. A browser test takes two guests through the lobby, the challenge and the race to both end cards and compares the two races' milestones: they match in Chromium and Firefox. WebKit is skipped because it needs a STUN server to connect. The machines have their own tests for admissions, races between commands, timeouts, the dispute and the result, both in the rules and against the stack's database.
  • Where it stopped. Two browsers connect only on the same machine or the same network: playing across the internet needs a STUN server and, behind some routers, a TURN relay, which no one has set up. Checking scores by replaying the race on the server also stopped. The rules can run the engine, but a single step of a server machine has a budget of a million operations, and replaying one duel takes 2.6 million. The record has the numbers.

Turn 39 · The race is the arena's: a kinetic child, a run machine, and no page script

Results. The fourth slice of the kinetic-machine record is built. The page that raced inside a frame is gone: the arena itself hosts the race as a Cortex kinetic child, and a run machine of rows says what the page shows. Apex Run serves no script of its own.

  • The child. The program declares the race once: its engine, its sound, its painter and its keys. The platform starts it on the arena and hands it the run row's seed, mode, keys and clock; it hands back each milestone and each menu press.
  • The run machine. Title, race, pause, the end, the initials and the save are the run row's phases, folded from those milestones and presses on one seat. The stage name, fork labels, route matrix, rival card, the cards and the board are markup over that row. The stage shown now changes as the arch is passed, before the haze.
  • On the canvas. The swoop's stage card, 3-2-1 and GO!, GOAL! and TIME OVER, the stage-clear card, the banners and the haze are drawn by the renderer's worker, looking as their CSS did.
  • Initials. The race's own key mapper types them: arrows, letters, Backspace, Enter, or a gamepad's stick. Once the race ends no key reaches the car; a test drives both a key and a gamepad axis through the platform's wiring to prove it.
  • Retired. The custom boot, the bridge, the initials helper, the showcase page and its script. The pit-lane rewind is the platform's own time-travel bar. The hot-seat duel is a title-card button now, since a query parameter cannot seed a row.
  • Where it stopped. The rival card stays up through the hand-over, the online duel waits for its duel machine, and the radio lasts the tab. The record lists each.

Turn 38 · Two planes: the sound in Jessie, milestones lifted out of the race, and the fast HUD in the worker

Results. The owner framed the page and the race as two planes: rows moved by IVM and drawn in HTML, one to many, over a race moved by rollback and drawn on canvas, few to few. The kinetic-machine record now decides how they meet, and three of its slices are built. The fourth, the run as a Cortex child that retires the custom boot, is designed and stopped.

  • The record. The DOM plane composes the race; the race reaches rows only by lifting its milestones, each once its frame is confirmed and with the commands since the last, so a row is never rolled back and a row can always overrule the race. The HUD splits by refresh rate; audio is a Jessie role; initials are a form.
  • The sound. The mix is a Jessie module the terminal hands one AudioContext, with worklets and other output devices taken off it first. A probe walks every value that context and its nodes expose and finds no page, network or storage. The noise bed now comes from a fixed seed.
  • Milestones. The run lifts its start, each checkpoint, time over and the finish, minutes apart, never per frame, two bytes of commands a frame. Every recorded run loads back from its rows alone to the forward run's checksum at each milestone. Two peers over a lossy, late channel lift the same rows only once GGRS has both inputs, for which GGRS's wrapper now reports its confirmed frame.
  • The fast HUD. Speed, revs, gauges, clock, score, radar and the rival's gap are drawn by the renderer's worker into canvases set where those digits sat, in the panel's own font, colour and glow; the panels stay the page's. At one fixed tick the HUD reads as before; the route matrix's card is a few pixels taller.
  • Where it stopped. Pause becomes the run row's, the countdown and banners join the canvas, the stage shown changes at the checkpoint, and the bridge becomes the terminal's. The record lists it; the boot, the bridge, the initials helpers and the showcase are what stays plain JavaScript.

Turn 37 · Firefox paints the car, the worker reaches no upstream, and two windows race a duel

Results. The Firefox car, the presenter's residuals, a live fork, and then 1v1 racing as pronto's kinetic-machine record lays it out: the duel, two players on one keyboard, and two windows racing over a data channel with GGRS. The lobby that pairs two people across the internet stopped where the record says.

  • The black car. Not Firefox's shader: the car's lacquer takes its diffuse light from the car probe, which reads its light back from the GPU after a capture, and a race that started before the read landed drew the paint black. Chromium's read happened to win. Each stage now lends the probe its sky's light until its own arrives, and a recapture swaps the new light in instead of emptying the old.
  • The worker's residuals. Its module imports of this site arrive as a script's fetch, and both doors now answer those from static files only, never from the cluster, whose GETs may act. It no longer connects to data:. 'unsafe-eval' and blob: stay, argued in the record. The edge release, which packaged no unit at all, now packages the presenter.
  • A live fork. Driven through the shell at /: left to Desert Canyons, right to Gateway Pass, and the castle to its goal, initials saved and the row arriving live on a second player's board.
  • The duel. Two cars on one stage, the first under the arch wins. Both race on the hero car's numbers; each player sees their own as the hero car. A recorded two-car duel joins the sync test.
  • Against a peer. GGRS, GGPO's rollback in Rust, builds byte for byte the same into 214 KiB of Wasm. It predicts the other car, and when the real input lands late, loads that frame and races forward again. The peers compare checksums every ten frames. Two windows race over a real data channel in Chromium and Firefox. WebKit's do not connect without a STUN server, the same wall as two homes.
  • Where it stopped. The lobby's offer and answer are rows a machine's fold reads, and the race lives in a page that only the plain-JavaScript boot can reach. Building the lobby now means more of that bridge; retiring the bridge first means the gaps the last turns listed. The record says so and what comes next.

Turn 36 · The race steps in Jessie, and the game runs in Firefox and WebKit

Results. Three asks: try the game in Firefox and Safari's engine, give Jessie an import on Endo's own tools rather than one of ours, and move the simulation into it.

  • Firefox and WebKit. Both refused to start the renderer's worker. They check a module worker's own imports against its worker-src, which said 'none'. It now says 'self'; a nested worker stays impossible because the worker's Worker is deleted. Every escape probe holds in both, and the boot gate passes in all three engines. Firefox draws with WebGPU in the worker but paints the car bodies black; WebKit has no WebGPU on this Mac (Safari's needs macOS 26) and draws with WebGL2. Playwright's builds are not shipping Safari or Firefox.
  • Jessie import. An engine is a graph of Jessie modules that import each other by relative path. When the app is generated, Endo's ModuleSource precompiles each one; in the page, SES's own module system links them in one cage, endowed with nothing. Nothing new ships to the browser. The link refuses import(), import.meta, packages, URLs, paths out of the app and any module reaching a denied name.
  • The race in the cage. The simulation, its PRNG and the stage layout are that graph. The sync test now runs on the caged engine, and each recorded run ends exactly where the plain module does. A step costs about 110 µs against 45 µs, under a hundredth of a frame. Initials, saving a score and the live board pass as before.
  • Still plain JavaScript. The boot, the bridge, the HUD, audio and the initials editor: what the record keeps for the kinetic machine the 1v1 race will need.

Turn 35 · The renderer paints in a worker that can do nothing else, and the rollback is tested every frame

Results. The owner asked that nothing in the game run as plain JavaScript beside the page: Jessie where pronto can check it, and state that only machines own. Babylon cannot run under SES (it writes onto Array.prototype.push), so the renderer went where it can only paint, and the rest stopped at a list of what the platform still lacks.

  • The renderer is a presenter. Pronto now has a second role for a worker unit: an agent without a memo is a presenter. Its worker gets a canvas, a policy that refuses every connection but blob: and data:, and globals pruned to what drawing needs, and it raises nothing. Babylon and its loaders arrive fetched against their integrity; the art arrives through the seat while the race holds. Every escape the spike tried still fails, now against the terminal's own worker.
  • The same picture, steadier frames. Shots at ticks 360, 700 and 1100 differ from the old build by no more than the old build differs from itself. Over 20 s of the first stage, 17–23 frames took over 25 ms, against 194–219 when the page drew them.
  • A bug only a worker could have. One run drew the road at a twentieth of its pixels: with no CSS size to read, Babylon scaled its own last drawing size at every adaptive step. The canvas is now fitted to its CSS size first, and a test holds it.
  • The rollback, every frame. The kinetic host has GGPO's sync test: after every tick it rewinds eight frames and re-simulates them, and the first frame that disagrees fails by number. Three recorded runs cover the title, both sides of the first fork, the arch, a time over and an ending. The renderer is a view: it keeps drawing when the host seeks backwards.
  • What stopped. The run as a cortex machine and the simulation as Jessie need six things the platform does not have yet, among them a HUD that updates 60 times a second and a machine for the run's phases. The record lists them; pronto's lint now names the five files still plain JavaScript here.

Turn 34 · The stage end runs into haze, a clean load, a gentler pull, and SuperTuxKart removed

Results. Three asks: the stage end that still drove into the void, the ghost car while loading, and a camera pull that was a bit too much. Then a cleanup: everything left from SuperTuxKart was removed.

  • The road runs on. A fork stage's road now continues 400 m past its end, with both branches at full divergence and the gore, edges, dashes and terrain beside them. A test drives 600 frames through a change of stage and fails any frame without road under the car and at least 250 m ahead. Over the last 160 m the world fades into haze: the fog thickens, and the painted sky, the distance and the clouds fade toward it. The next stage fades back in over its first 240 m, and the switch happens inside the haze.
  • The freeze at the switch. It was 850 ms. Now one frame of 67–117 ms remains, inside full haze, and the frames after it run at 17–33 ms. Five changes got it there:
    • The next stages' clouds are painted a few rows a frame while the current stage is driven.
    • One environment texture takes each stage's sky, and the post-process keeps its own image settings, so the switch no longer marks every material dirty.
    • Traffic cars share each type's materials and are pooled per slot and type, built one a frame ahead.
    • The verge's models and scatter are built one job a frame after the switch.
    What remains is the road, the terrain and the props being rebuilt (about 50 ms), plus their first draw (about 55 ms).
  • No ghost car. The game is hidden behind APEX RUN · LOADING until its first frame is drawn with the title card up, and only then fades in.
  • A gentler pull. At a landmark the camera now moves 7.8 m back and 3.9 m up, and swings 2.7 m out. The lens widens 0.094 rad and the aim turns 47% of the way to the landmark. That is about a fifth less than before, over the same length.
  • SuperTuxKart removed.
    • The item system: item boxes, the fire input, zipper, bowling balls, bananas, the parachute and kart rank, with their telemetry, preset, sound cues and renderer pools.
    • The kart game's Match and Kart state. Its fields are retired in place, as LapRecord's were.
    • The referee and session_control handlers, with their flows and checks.
    • The old track aliases and an unused character.
    A stage's segments are now built once rather than every tick, and a particle texture that fails to paint now fails loudly. A new README says where the game came from.

Turn 32 · Ghibli gouache, painted surfaces baked onto the props

Results. Colour was done; what still separated the frame from the concepts was surface. The props were smooth, flat-shaded models where the concepts' are painted.

  • A baker. The new tool in assets/tools/ghibli/bake renders a model from five views and repaints each view as gouache with SDXL. It then projects the paintings back onto the model as one painted texture per level of detail. It carries only what each painting adds to its render, the brushwork and some warm and cool shifts, over the model's own colour. Taking the paintings' colours whole had produced a patchwork, and a palm trunk turned green.
  • Baked. The desert's buttes and the palms went first, then the firs, cypress, broadleaf trees, sea stacks, column and broken arch. The castle, colosseum and hangar already carry pack textures and keep them. On a painted surface the toon light only modulates, with no extra strokes laid over it.
  • A poppy carpet. The tulip road's verge is a painted flower carpet, so it reads as a mass at speed.
  • Smaller fixes. The power line has its wires. Gateway Pass's cypress avenue stands back from the road, and the gateway's limestone no longer reads mauve. The sunset road's red earth is tinted cooler.

Gates. sayt lint, sayt test and sayt integrate pass on every commit. 60 fps at 1920×1080 at DPR 2 on eight stages after each slice, painted textures included: p99 17.5–17.7 ms, resolution scale 0.5–0.56. Open: the painted surfaces are subtle at driving distance, a grain of brushwork rather than the concepts' painted forms; SDXL at a strength that keeps the silhouette paints smooth. Gateway Pass's land measures 0.18, up from 0.11 but short of 0.27. The sunset earth's saturation barely moved (0.53). The arch keeps the stage's violet dusk.
